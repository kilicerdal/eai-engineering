> A very small painting gives improbable objects the clarity of evidence. The landscape appears stable while the things used to measure time lose their resistance.

## A small canvas made in 1931, acquired in 1934 | history | 1 | section-1

MoMA dates this oil painting to 1931 and records an anonymous gift in 1934. Its modest physical dimensions are essential to the encounter: a reproduction enlarged to fill a monitor can make it seem monumental. The institutional record situates it within Dalí’s Surrealist practice; that classification does not establish a single meaning for its clocks.

## A stable horizon holds unstable objects | analysis | 1 | section-2

Begin with the relationship between the near plane and the distant cliffs. The horizon gives the eye a familiar spatial anchor. Against that reassurance, the soft watches interrupt the distinction between a rigid manufactured object and yielding organic matter. The impossible event is persuasive because surrounding surfaces continue to behave with descriptive consistency.

## The pale central form resists a secure name | analysis | 1 | section-3

The pale form at the center is difficult to name securely. A closed eye and eyelash-like forms encourage a bodily reading, while its low position and slack contour make it resemble something left on the ground. Rather than deciding immediately whether it is a portrait, trace the contour and notice where recognition becomes uncertain. That hesitation is an observable viewing experience, not proof of a particular dream.

## Hard edges and soft bodies alter the pace of looking | analysis | 1 | section-4

The separation of hard and soft forms can produce an uneven tempo of looking. Sharp edges bring the gaze to a stop; sagging contours send it downward. Consider how a watch draped over an edge both repeats that edge and denies its firmness. Time is not simply illustrated by the presence of clocks. Its ordinary measuring instruments are placed in a material world where their authority becomes doubtful.

## Later anecdotes are not contemporary evidence | interpretation | 1 | section-5

Familiar stories connecting the clocks to cheese or to scientific theories should be attributed and dated before being used as explanations. A memorable later anecdote is not equivalent to a contemporary account of the painting’s execution.

## Minute description and material impossibility | technical | 1 | section-6

The recorded medium is oil on canvas, and the stated dimensions are 24.1 × 33 cm.

The small oil painting asks the eye to move close. Precise boundaries and apparently smooth modeling lend credibility to objects whose behavior is impossible; the persuasive finish is part of the contradiction, rather than a neutral vehicle for a symbol.

## The authority of small things | analysis | 1 | section-7

The painting’s small dimensions alter the kind of attention it asks for. The forms are not encountered at the scale of actual clocks in an expansive environment; they are compressed into an object a viewer approaches. The contrast between that intimate physical scale and the apparently extensive landscape is worth retaining. When the digital image fills a large screen, the landscape can dominate and the concentrated workmanship can become merely an effect of resolution. The record of dimensions restores a different possibility of looking.

The hardness of the ledge and the softness of the watches make each other perceptible. A yielding form would be less surprising without a boundary over which it could sag. Conversely, the firm edge becomes especially insistent when another object seems unable to maintain its own shape. This reciprocal organization is more precise than a broad claim that the painting shows time becoming fluid. The title and clocks invite that interpretation, but the effect is made through concrete contrasts of material behavior.

The sparse environment also controls the spectator’s search for causes. There is little ordinary activity through which the strange objects might be explained. Shadows and distant formations offer a coherent setting while withholding an event that could account for what lies before us. The viewer receives descriptive certainty and causal uncertainty at the same time. That combination is a central source of the picture’s power.

## Making, acquisition and later testimony | history | 1 | section-8

The work’s museum acquisition in 1934 belongs to a different chronology from its making in 1931. Keeping the two moments apart prevents a familiar masterpiece from appearing to have existed outside professional circulation. No exact first-owner sequence or restoration history is supplied here without the relevant record. For the same reason, a later explanation of a motif must be identified as later testimony. It may illuminate the artist’s presentation of the work without replacing a direct account of the surface or becoming a contemporary diary of its creation.

## Return to the work | analysis |  | return-to-the-work

Look once more from the distant cliffs to the nearest soft watch. The setting offers order while the object frustrates it: descriptive confidence can make uncertainty more persistent.
