# A Storm: a sudden incision

> A sharp bolt cuts through a soft field of darkness. In this pastel, O’Keeffe makes the contrast between abrupt event and surrounding atmosphere carry the experience of a storm.

## A work on paper beside the paintings | history | 1

The Met dates A Storm to 1922 and records its medium as pastel on paper mounted on illustration board. It entered the collection as an anonymous gift in 1981. This is a work on paper, not an oil painting, and is labeled accordingly within the broader collection of studies. The museum associates the scene, cautiously, with O’Keeffe’s experience at Lake George. That qualified association should remain a possibility rather than being converted into a documented date and location for a particular weather event.

## A sudden incision | analysis |

The lightning makes a jagged, concentrated interruption in a broad dark field. Its warmer color distinguishes it from the surrounding blues, so contrast works through temperature as well as brightness. Trace the bolt’s changes of direction before looking at the lower bands. The event is compressed into a narrow path while its setting extends laterally. That disparity makes the lightning feel decisive: a small amount of the surface appears capable of changing the atmosphere of the whole.

## Atmosphere and the hand-made surface | technical | 1

Pastel’s appearance can differ markedly from a smoothly glazed oil painting. In the reproduction, dark areas suggest a soft, smudged density against which the sharper bolt stands out. This is a visual description, not a laboratory finding about how every layer was applied. The mounted support also matters to the identity and care of the object. A screen image cannot reproduce the particulate surface or establish how it has changed. Consult the collection record before inferring a specific technical procedure.

## Water, light and uncertainty | analysis |

The lower areas introduce another kind of light, broader and less directional than the central bolt. They make the boundary between water, land and sky worth investigating rather than instantly obvious. Compare the crescent-like light at the edges with the angular event in the center. The picture’s drama depends on these unequal forms. A viewer can describe their shapes and relations with confidence while remaining cautious about assigning an exact physical explanation to every illuminated passage.

## More than a weather illustration | interpretation |

The work can be approached as a distilled experience of natural forces rather than an account designed to identify a particular meteorological system. O’Keeffe selects the opposition of abrupt line and enveloping field. That selection makes the storm a problem of pictorial rhythm. It is unnecessary to claim that the image is entirely abstract or entirely descriptive. Its interest lies in how recognizability survives the reduction of descriptive detail, and how the formal reduction increases the event’s emotional intensity.

## Why include a pastel here? | interpretation |

Placed beside Blue and Green Music and Black Iris, A Storm makes comparison across media possible. Look for the relationship between a strong boundary and a surrounding field in each work. Similar questions do not imply identical effects or identical working methods. This compact selection introduces part of O’Keeffe’s early career without pretending to represent her complete development. It also creates a useful threshold for future drawings and studies: works on paper can be examined as finished objects, not merely as incomplete versions of paintings.
