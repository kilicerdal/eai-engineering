> Self-Portrait as Saint Catherine of Alexandria connects personal likeness, learned attributes and the public authority of a martyr.

## Likeness within a religious role | history | 1 | section-1

Saint Catherine is identified through attributes associated with her martyrdom, including the wheel and palm. The National Gallery understands the image as Gentileschi using her own likeness in the saint’s role. Such an image should not be reduced either to a conventional devotional figure or to a straightforward modern self-portrait.

The religious identity offered a recognized language of conviction and endurance. The artist’s likeness participates in that language, but it does not establish that every element is a coded statement about one biographical event.

## Directness and the weight of objects | analysis |  | section-2

The face addresses the viewer while the body and attributes establish a more complicated set of directions. The wheel is not merely a label inserted at the edge: its hard, damaged form contrasts with flesh and fabric, giving the saint’s history a material presence.

Light concentrates attention on the head and the active hands. The costume expands the figure’s silhouette, while the close framing denies a broad narrative setting. The result is an encounter with a person performing an identity rather than a distant scene populated by anonymous actors.

## Professional self-representation | interpretation |  | section-3

The picture demonstrates how an artist can use an established subject to articulate a public image. Gentileschi represents a woman whose authority is bound to conviction, and she does so through a recognizably accomplished handling of likeness, drapery and light.

Its force lies in the relationship among those achievements. The painting does not need to be read as an unmediated confession to carry personal significance. It is a deliberately constructed meeting of authorship, religious role and professional skill.
