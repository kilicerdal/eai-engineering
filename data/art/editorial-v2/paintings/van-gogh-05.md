# A room whose colors refuse repose
> The Night Café makes a public interior feel exposed and unstable through forceful color relations and a perspective that draws the viewer toward its center.

## An interior with a stated expressive aim | history | 1,2
Van Gogh painted the café interior in Arles in 1888. His correspondence discusses the expressive use of clashing colors in this work. The painting can therefore be considered alongside an artist’s account of deliberate effects, rather than treated only as an accidental reflection of his condition.

Unlike the outdoor Café Terrace at Night, this scene encloses the viewer within a room. Its inhabitants occupy different positions of stillness and withdrawal, while the furnishings hold an unusually assertive place in the composition.
## The billiard table as a spatial instrument | analysis |
The table projects strongly toward the foreground, drawing attention into a space whose red and green areas remain in tension. Lamps form bright circular concentrations above, and their halos make illumination feel insistent rather than gentle.

The floor’s diagonals and the isolated figures create a room that seems both open and socially disconnected. The attendant stands apart, while seated people do not form a coherent conversation. The eye is offered routes through the space without the reassurance of a unified social center.
## Color can make a setting argumentative | interpretation |
The picture does not merely describe the appearance of an establishment. It uses that setting to construct an emotional environment. Red, green, yellow and the exaggerated spatial directions make the room act upon the spectator.

This is a crucial distinction in Van Gogh’s practice: expressive meaning can be created through formal relationships without requiring symbols that translate one-to-one into a verbal message. The café remains recognizable, but its color and perspective make recognition insufficient. The viewer has to experience how the room has been made to feel.
