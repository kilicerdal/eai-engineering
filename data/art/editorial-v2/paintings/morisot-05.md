# A mirror and the act of self-presentation
> A woman adjusts her clothing before a tall dressing mirror. Morisot makes this small act of self-presentation a study of who looks, what can be seen, and how private appearance is prepared.
@source Museo Thyssen-Bornemisza · Psyche Mirror|https://www.museothyssen.org/en/collection/artists/morisot-berthe/psyche-mirror|1876 work, mirror terminology and 1877 exhibition
## The title names an object | history | 1

A tall tilting dressing mirror was known as a psyché. The title does not by itself turn the woman into the mythological Psyche. Painted in 1876 and shown in the 1877 Impressionist exhibition, the work gives a contemporary domestic activity a place within the movement’s public presentation.

The woman adjusts the fabric at her waist while looking toward the mirror. The museum offers a qualified interpretation involving the effect of a corset; that possibility should remain a possibility rather than a fully documented account of her thoughts.
## Whites differentiated by light | analysis |

The dress, reflected surfaces and surrounding room create a range of pale tones. Light separates them without requiring strongly bounded outlines. The figure’s turn connects the visible body to the implied act of evaluating its reflected appearance.

The mirror complicates the relation between the person and the spectator. She is engaged in looking, not merely being looked at. Furniture and framing establish a private room, but the painter’s viewpoint lets us observe the work involved in presenting a body.
## Intimacy is not the absence of social pressure | interpretation |

The scene can be considered in relation to dress, gender and self-regard without reducing it to an automatic allegory of vanity. The woman’s own attention is active. At the same time, self-presentation occurs within social expectations about appearance.

Morisot gives this ordinary negotiation formal precision. The painting makes a private adjustment into a structure of distinct viewpoints, demonstrating how a domestic subject can examine the relation between a person’s agency and the image they are expected to produce.

Look once more at the turn of the figure and the direction of her attention. We can observe an adjustment and a relationship to the mirror; we cannot observe a thought. Keeping that distinction makes the painting more demanding. The spectator must consider the labor of appearance without pretending to possess the woman’s private judgment of herself. The mirror offers access to another view while preserving the distance between looking at a person and knowing her.
