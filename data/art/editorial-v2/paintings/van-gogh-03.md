> The Bedroom makes color, simplified form and deliberately unsettled perspective work together in an image whose intended calm is more complicated than it first appears.

## This room, this version | history | 1,2 | section-1

The Amsterdam painting is the 1888 version of Van Gogh’s room in the Yellow House at Arles. He subsequently made other versions, now in different collections. Their colors and details are not interchangeable. His letters describe the importance of color and the desire to communicate rest through the picture.

The room was part of his effort to establish a working life in Arles. That biographical context is useful, but the image is not simply a floor plan or an untouched record of furniture arrangement. Its forms have been actively simplified and related.

## The perspective refuses perfect agreement | analysis |  | section-2

The bed occupies a large part of the right side, while chairs, table and wall pictures form smaller units around it. Lines draw the eye inward but do not all produce one smoothly reconciled spatial system. The room feels close and legible, yet slightly unstable.

Broad color areas make objects emphatic without extensive modeling. The wall pictures repeat the larger act of representation within the room, suggesting a life surrounded by images. The empty floor creates space for an absent inhabitant without supplying a figure to explain the mood.

## Intention and experience can differ | interpretation |  | section-3

Knowing that Van Gogh associated the work with rest does not require every viewer to experience uncomplicated calm. The strong outlines and shifting perspective can create tension alongside the simplicity. That difference is part of the painting’s interest, not a reason to dismiss the artist’s account.

The picture demonstrates how a personal environment can become a formal experiment. Color and arrangement attempt to give a room emotional consequence, making habitation visible even in the inhabitant’s absence.
