# Girl before a Mirror · a sustained looking study

> A figure reaches toward a reflection that does not simply repeat her. Curved divisions, saturated color and emphatic outlines turn resemblance into a negotiation between surface and imagined interior.

## A dated encounter with Marie-Thérèse Walter | history | 1 | section-1

MoMA records the exact date March 14, 1932, Paris, and identifies the work with Picasso’s representations of Marie-Thérèse Walter. The painting entered the museum through the gift of Mrs. Simon Guggenheim in 1938. The sitter’s identity is documented in the museum account; the psychological meanings assigned to her doubled image require a separate argument.

## The reaching arm bridges two unequal appearances | analysis | 1 | section-2

The oval mirror establishes an enclosure within the canvas. Follow the arm that bridges the figure and its reflected counterpart: the gesture promises contact, but the two zones do not behave as a mechanically consistent optical system. The border of the mirror is therefore more than furniture. It is a device for arranging different kinds of depiction within a single field.

## Color divides the face rather than explaining a mood | analysis | 1 | section-3

Color can be read as a construction of bodily volume and as a refusal of ordinary flesh. The face is divided, the torso patterned, and the reflection darker and differently articulated. These differences make a literal comparison possible without settling its meaning. A viewer may associate the changes with time, fear or self-scrutiny, but should first describe which changes in contour and color support that association.

## Pattern competes with the figure | analysis | 1 | section-4

The patterned background competes with the body rather than providing neutral space. It is worth temporarily ignoring facial expression and following repeated curves instead. Their recurrence connects figure, mirror and setting. The surface then reads as one organized structure, even as the subject proposes a division between an outward appearance and another image of the self.

## A named sitter does not settle the reflection | interpretation | 1 | section-5

MoMA’s identification of the sitter should not be confused with evidence that a particular color records her emotion. Read symbolic accounts as interpretations, and resist treating Picasso’s perspective as the sitter’s own testimony.

## Oil paint makes incompatible surfaces coexist | technical | 1 | section-6

The recorded medium is oil on canvas, and the stated dimensions are 162.3 × 130.2 cm.

The repeated outlines carry a different kind of authority from softly graded flesh. Color can assert the body’s presence while refusing to make its reflection obey the same description. Compare an enclosed patch on the figure with a corresponding patch inside the mirror.

## Reflection as a second pictorial language | analysis | 1 | section-7

A mirror normally authorizes an explanation: one body produces two appearances according to a consistent optical relation. Here that expectation becomes a point of departure. Differences in contour and color make the reflection an active counterpart rather than a passive confirmation. It may seem to expose another condition of the sitter, but that impression is an effect constructed for a viewer. We do not gain access to Marie-Thérèse Walter’s private understanding merely by recognizing her image.

The reach toward the mirror is especially consequential. It places a gesture of connection across a boundary that the surrounding pictorial languages keep asserting. If the reflection appears alien or troubling, the hand complicates a simple division between an acceptable exterior and a rejected interior. The scene can sustain attraction, curiosity or ambivalence without requiring us to settle which emotion is its definitive content. What matters first is that contact is proposed across a visibly differentiated field.

The figure’s patterned environment is part of the argument, not an ornamental distraction. Repeated curves and enclosed shapes make the sitter seem connected to the whole surface even as the mirror divides that surface into regions. This double operation produces a useful tension: the subject proposes an individual confronting herself, while the composition treats body and setting as interdependent painted forms. Follow that relationship before assigning fixed meanings to particular hues.

## The museum record and the limits of biography | history | 1 | section-8

The museum’s gift credit establishes a documented institutional stage in the work’s journey. It should not be expanded into a full history of purchase, first display or the sitter’s response without further documents. Likewise, a widely repeated psychological interpretation may belong to the painting’s reception rather than its original circumstances. The distinction makes the study more exact: a documented person, a dated object and an argument about reflection can be considered together without being treated as the same kind of evidence.

## Return to the work | analysis |  | return-to-the-work

Return to the hand that reaches toward the mirror. It joins the two appearances without making them equivalent; that unresolved connection is more useful than assigning a fixed emotional meaning to every color.
