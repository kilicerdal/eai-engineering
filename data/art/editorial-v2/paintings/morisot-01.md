# Care and the boundary around it
> The Cradle makes maternal attention a relation of bodies and sight, while the translucent veil creates a boundary the viewer cannot simply ignore.

## A family subject in a public artistic career | history | 1,2
Morisot painted her sister Edma watching her infant daughter in 1872. The work entered the public history of Impressionism through the first independent exhibition in 1874. A private family subject therefore became part of a professional challenge to established exhibition structures.

Edma had also trained as an artist. This context complicates any assumption that the picture merely confirms an uncomplicated, timeless maternal role. The sisters’ different lives belong to the opportunities and restrictions surrounding women’s work in their social world.
## Repeated gestures, unequal positions | analysis |
The mother’s bent arm and the child’s small arm establish a subtle visual echo, but the two figures do not occupy equivalent spaces. The cradle’s gauze separates the infant from the spectator. Morisot paints a surface that can be seen through without disappearing.

Edma’s downward gaze gives the composition a concentrated direction. Dark clothing anchors her figure against the lighter bedding, while the veil draws a diagonal between the adult and the child. The viewer follows this attention but remains outside its most intimate circuit.
## Intimacy with a threshold | interpretation |
The picture can evoke tenderness, fatigue or absorption. Its visible arrangement supports a serious consideration of care without allowing us to claim a complete account of Edma’s feelings. The veil is important because it makes closeness coexist with restraint.

Morisot gives domestic experience formal and public consequence. The painting’s subject is not small because its room is small. It makes the distribution of attention, protection and personal space worthy of the same exacting consideration as more conventionally prestigious narratives.
