# Repetition makes differences visible
> Several Circles uses one recurring shape to produce a remarkably varied experience of weight, transparency, depth and suspension.
@source Guggenheim · Several Circles|https://www.guggenheim.org/artwork/1992|1926 work and the significance of circles
## A limited vocabulary with expansive possibilities | history | 1
Painted in 1926, the work belongs to Kandinsky’s Bauhaus years. The Guggenheim connects the recurring circle to his interest in reconciling oppositions through form. That theoretical context explains why such a restricted motif could be an ambitious subject rather than a simple reduction of effort.

The circles are not identified as a literal astronomical chart. Cosmic associations are possible, but the painting’s structure can be described without assigning every form the name of a planet or celestial body.
## Dark space and unequal spheres | analysis |
The circles differ in size, color and apparent opacity. Some overlap, creating new areas whose effects make the viewer imagine transparent layers. Others remain isolated against the dark field, acquiring a different degree of presence.

The largest forms establish a broad balance, while small circles interrupt it locally. Soft edges and brighter halos complicate the relation between flat geometry and an impression of luminous volume. The background feels spacious because the shapes activate it through unequal intervals.
## Simplicity is not sameness | interpretation |
The work demonstrates how repetition can sharpen perception. Because the basic shape recurs, differences of placement and color become especially consequential. A small change can alter a form’s apparent weight or its relation to the whole.

Kandinsky gives abstraction an unusual calm without making it inert. The image remains full of potential movement, but its forces are held in suspension. Its character arises from exact distinctions within a shared vocabulary, making restraint a source of complexity rather than an escape from it.
