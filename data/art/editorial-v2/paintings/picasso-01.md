> Five figures occupy a compressed interior. Faces do not share one descriptive language, and the angular planes make the surrounding space press forward rather than recede. The encounter concerns the viewer’s position as much as the represented bodies.

## A 1907 canvas and its later institutional authority | history | 1 | section-1

MoMA dates the painting to June–July 1907 in Paris and records its acquisition through the Lillie P. Bliss Bequest by exchange in 1939. Preparatory work belongs to a longer period of reconsideration. The museum discusses its relation to African sculpture and masks; that context includes colonial collecting, not a neutral exchange between equal institutions.

## Angular bodies resist continuous modeling | analysis | 1 | section-2

The bodies refuse a single smooth progression from light into shadow. Angular changes can make a limb seem constructed from separate surfaces. Rather than searching for anatomically correct volume, attend to the sequence by which your eye recognizes a torso, loses its continuity and reconstructs it. The surrounding fragments participate in that work: background and body are not cleanly separated into passive setting and active subject.

## An encounter that refuses a comfortable distance | analysis | 1 | section-3

The figures’ address makes distance difficult to maintain. Several look outward, while the seated figure complicates the relation between a turned body and a confronting face. That discrepancy puts the spectator in a position of uncertainty. Is the picture an invitation, an accusation or a demonstration of pictorial power? Each is an interpretive proposal. None follows automatically from the fact that the figures look toward us.

## The still life advances toward the viewer | analysis | 1 | section-4

The small still-life group at the bottom creates another threshold. Its pointed forms can appear to advance into the viewer’s space even as the figures flatten into the surface. Compare that local projection with the refusal of a stable room. The painting can produce force without providing a coherent stage, and its confrontation depends on that contradiction rather than on formal innovation alone.

## Sexual setting, representation and the modernist canon | interpretation | 1 | section-5

The relationship between the work’s sexual setting, its treatment of women and its later status as a founding modernist masterpiece remains a critical question. The museum’s account of African sources is a starting point, not an identification of every borrowed form or its maker.

## Painted planes at a bodily scale | technical | 1 | section-6

The recorded medium is oil on canvas, and the stated dimensions are 243.9 × 233.7 cm.

At more than two meters in each dimension, the canvas makes the confrontation bodily as well as optical. Oil paint here constructs angular transitions that can read as both modeling and a break in modeling; follow a cheek into its adjacent plane before deciding that all the faces use one system.

## The pressure of a constructed encounter | analysis | 1 | section-7

The painting makes several ordinarily cooperative conventions pull against one another. A figure can be frontal without occupying a stable frontal space. A facial contour can announce a recognizable head while resisting the modeled volume normally attached to it. The viewer is therefore not simply presented with unusual appearances; the viewer is made to perform the work of holding those appearances together. That effort is an important part of the encounter’s force. It gives the surface a resistance that a quick account of broken perspective can miss.

The represented women also make a purely formal account insufficient. Their address is inseparable from the sexualized situation and from the power of the person who constructs and controls the image. Admiring the arrangement does not require treating its subjects as neutral raw material. A serious reading should ask who is granted a position of mastery, how that position is challenged, and whether the painting’s aggression belongs to the figures, the artist’s representation or the viewer’s own experience. These alternatives cannot be resolved by a stylistic label.

The move into a museum collection gave this large, difficult canvas a different kind of public authority. Its eventual status as a point of origin for modern art can tempt us to read every earlier study as a step toward an inevitable result. Preparation should instead be understood as evidence of choices that might have gone otherwise. A finished work does not make its own route of development necessary.

## Museum recognition and the problem of origins | history | 1 | section-8

The MoMA acquisition record belongs to the painting’s institutional history, not to its moment of making in 1907. Its later position in histories of modernism should not make every preparatory decision appear inevitable. The complete ownership sequence is not transcribed here; follow the museum record rather than treating an unfilled interval as an uninterrupted chain.
