# A god with the presence of a model
> Bacchus makes classical divinity feel close, bodily and offered to the viewer, while refusing a perfectly polished ideal.
@source Uffizi Galleries · Bacchus|https://www.uffizi.it/en/artworks/caravaggio-bacchus|Current dating, early Roman context and documented interpretations
## Antiquity in an early Roman painting | history | 1
The Uffizi currently dates this painting to about 1598. It belongs to Caravaggio’s early Roman work and the cultural world of Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte. The god of wine is presented as a half-length figure with fruit and a glass, bringing a classical subject into the immediate scale of an encounter.

The museum records several critical readings, including convivial invitation and the sensuality of antiquity. These are interpretations, not a single surviving explanation dictated by the painter. The picture’s power partly depends on its ability to sustain more than one register: myth, still life and the presence of a living sitter.
## Invitation and resistance | analysis |
The offered glass is the key action. It bridges the space between the figure and an implied recipient, making the viewer a potential participant. The wreath and pale drapery establish the classical identity, but the face and body remain insistently particular rather than becoming an abstract emblem.

The fruit requires a different kind of attention. Individual surfaces and irregularities hold the eye against the larger movement of the arm. Looking moves between the offered drink, the face and the objects, so the invitation is repeatedly delayed by the pleasure of inspection.
## Naturalism does not mean simplicity | interpretation |
Calling the work realistic describes only one part of its operation. A recognizable body is being staged as a god; apparently ordinary objects acquire meaning through that staging. The painting is neither an unmediated record of a person nor a straightforward transcription of an antique statue.

Its distinction lies in this productive instability. Divinity is brought near enough to seem available, yet the theatrical arrangement remains visible. Caravaggio uses the credibility of surfaces to make an invented situation persuasive, and then leaves the viewer to decide how welcoming, sensual or unsettling that situation feels.
