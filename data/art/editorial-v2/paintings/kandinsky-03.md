> Yellow–Red–Blue gives its named colors different territories, then complicates those territories through lines and forms that refuse a simple division.

## A mature Bauhaus investigation | history | 1 | section-1

The 1925 painting belongs to Kandinsky’s sustained study of relationships among colors and forms. Its title identifies elements of the pictorial vocabulary rather than a narrative subject. The work participates in his theoretical ambitions while remaining a specific arrangement that cannot be replaced by a general diagram of color associations.

The historical context matters because Kandinsky did not understand abstraction as arbitrary decoration. He sought a language capable of producing effects through relations among visual elements. The painting tests that ambition in a large, varied composition.

## Territories that do not remain separate | analysis |  | section-2

A warmer, more angular area at the left contrasts with a broad blue circular form at the right. Red and smaller colored elements mediate without simply occupying a passive middle. Black lines cross, bend and connect regions, giving each area more than one direction.

The ground shifts in tone rather than remaining an empty support. This makes the shapes seem to press, float or recede differently. The eye can follow a dominant opposition across the canvas, then discover smaller relations that complicate it.

## Theory meets an irreducible painting | interpretation |  | section-3

Kandinsky’s ideas can guide attention, but no theoretical statement fully substitutes for the encounter. The yellow and blue do not act in isolation; their effects depend on scale, surrounding colors and the forms they inhabit.

The painting’s sophistication lies in the difference between a proposed vocabulary and a living composition. A few named colors become an extensive field of decisions. Their interaction produces tension and balance without resolving into either a conventional scene or a purely mechanical system.
