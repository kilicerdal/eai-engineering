> A seated woman gathers light at the edge of a working harbor. Morisot brings an intimate foreground and a more distant world of boats, water and buildings into one field of attention.

## An early encounter with a coastal place | history | 1,2 | section-1

Painted in 1869, the work belongs to Morisot’s early career, before the first independent Impressionist exhibition. The seated woman is associated with her sister Edma. A recognizable family presence is placed within a harbor environment rather than isolated against an abstract portrait background.

The waterfront carries a scale of activity different from the quiet foreground. Boats, buildings and water connect the scene to travel and work, while the seated figure gives the viewer a near, human measure of the place.

## A figure that does not dominate the whole view | analysis |  | section-2

The woman’s pale clothing gathers light and provides a clear foreground form. Behind her, the harbor extends through a sequence of horizontal and diagonal relationships. The picture gives her prominence without allowing her body to erase the environment.

Architecture and rigging introduce more angular rhythms than the folds of the dress. These differences make the two zones of the composition distinct. The viewer can move between a person at rest and a setting whose forms imply movement and practical activity.

## The relation is the subject | interpretation |  | section-3

The painting is neither simply a portrait with scenery nor simply a landscape containing a scale figure. Its interest lies in the proportion of attention granted to each. A private relation and a public place occupy one field without becoming interchangeable meanings.

Morisot’s early work already demonstrates an ability to make such negotiations subtle. The scene’s apparent quiet depends on a careful balance of proximity and distance, with the seated body acting as an anchor from which the harbor remains open to exploration.

Move from the folds of the pale dress to the angular lines beyond it, then return to the seated figure. The change in rhythm makes the near and distant worlds distinct without breaking the picture apart. Her presence gives the harbor a bodily scale; the harbor prevents her presence from becoming the painting’s only subject. This exchange is more specific than a general claim about Impressionist modernity. It describes how Morisot makes a private encounter remain open to a place beyond it.
