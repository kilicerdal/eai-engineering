# The False Mirror · a sustained looking study

> An eye becomes a place where a sky appears. The image asks whether vision gives access to the world, constructs it, or becomes an object under another gaze.

@source Museum of Modern Art, New York · The False Mirror | https://www.moma.org/collection/works/78938 | Object identity, date, medium and institutional account. Visual readings below are original editorial analysis, not museum quotations.

## The particular object | history | 1

MoMA currently dates this work Paris 1929 and records an acquisition in 1935. Older references sometimes give 1928; this study follows the current holding institution and preserves the discrepancy as a source-maintenance question rather than quietly treating all dates as equivalent. The museum record concerns this object, not every later eye image made by Magritte.

## How the image organizes attention | analysis | 1

The eye occupies most of the field, leaving little surrounding face to establish an individual identity. The enlarged scale makes a familiar organ strange without making it unrecognizable. Attend to the transition between the eyelids and the sky. The boundary proposes a contained interior while the clouds suggest an expanse that cannot normally fit inside it.

## A second relationship | analysis | 1

The black pupil remains a concentrated interruption within the cloudy field. It can look like an opening, a flat disk or an opaque refusal of information. These are different perceptual possibilities. Try each in turn and notice how it changes the relationship between a body that sees and a surface that is seen.

## Space, surface and the viewer | analysis | 1

The title encourages a relation to reflection, but the image does not behave as a straightforward mirror. A real reflected sky would depend on light and the eye’s curved surface; this painted sky replaces much of the iris with another pictorial field. Magritte turns a supposed instrument of direct access into a constructed image that complicates access itself.

## What remains an argument | interpretation | 1

The discrepancy between 1928 in older descriptions and 1929 in MoMA’s present record requires reference to catalogue evidence, not a vote between websites. Interpretations of the eye as surveillance or modern media are useful contemporary readings when labeled as such, not established predictions by Magritte.

## Materials and the limits of a reproduction | technical | 1

The recorded medium is oil on canvas, and the stated dimensions are 54 × 80.9 cm. These identify the object’s support and scale; they do not, by themselves, reveal the exact sequence of execution. A statement about underdrawing, pigment chemistry or a concealed earlier state requires a specific technical examination. This study does not infer those findings from a photograph.

## Seeing becomes something to inspect | analysis | 1

A painted eye can place a viewer under an imagined gaze. The image may seem to look back even though its enormous pupil supplies no identifiable person behind it. That combination of apparent address and missing identity makes the usual relation between spectator and portrait uncertain. We see an eye without acquiring the biographical confidence that a conventional face might invite.

The cloudy field complicates the eye’s expected interior. Instead of a patterned iris surrounding an aperture, the spectator encounters a pictured environment around a black center. Ask whether the center appears to interrupt the sky, open behind it or lie on its surface. Each possibility gives the image a different organization. The painting’s uncertainty is therefore not merely a subject to name; it is an effect a viewer can test by changing the relation imagined between those forms.

## A public life without an invented chain | history | 1

The title offers a verbal complication rather than a reliable technical description of reflection. A mirror suggests the return of an external appearance, while this eye presents an expansive image where the structure of an iris might be expected. It is useful to keep those operations apart. Otherwise the title can make a viewer overlook what the painted relation actually does.

MoMA’s current 1929 dating is retained with an editorial flag for older 1928 descriptions. That flag is not a declaration that every older source is worthless. It identifies a question requiring catalogue evidence and a clear record of revision. Similarly, the 1935 acquisition belongs to the particular canvas’s collecting history, not to all later images of eyes in Magritte’s practice. Exact identification prevents a compelling philosophical account from floating free of the object that occasioned it.
