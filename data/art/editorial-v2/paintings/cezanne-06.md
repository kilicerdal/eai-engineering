# An image of objects, an object among images
> A pale plaster Cupid stands among objects and other pictures. Cézanne gives the cast bodily weight while making the surrounding floor, supports and represented spaces difficult to reconcile.
@source Courtauld · Still Life with Plaster Cupid|https://gallerycollections.courtauld.ac.uk/object-p-1948-sc-59|Work record and studio objects
## A studio as a field of representations | history | 1

Cézanne’s still life includes a plaster figure traditionally identified as Cupid, together with surrounding objects and images. A cast is already a representation of a body, so painting it introduces a further level of mediation. The subject is not simply nature arranged on a table; it is a studio populated by forms made and seen in different ways.

The work belongs to Cézanne’s sustained practice of using still life to investigate pictorial relations. Its objects are modest in status but unusually demanding in their spatial arrangement.
## The floor will not stay securely flat | analysis |

The cast supplies a pale vertical mass whose small body contrasts with the spread of objects around it. Tilted surfaces and overlapping frames make the transition between floor, tabletop and painted backgrounds difficult to resolve.

The eye encounters several kinds of space at once. A represented object occupies the studio, while nearby paintings contain their own represented worlds. Cézanne does not entirely reconcile these levels into a seamless illusion. Their friction remains visible.
## Painting examines its own conditions | interpretation |

The image can be read as an inquiry into how objects become forms within a picture. The plaster Cupid is solid, but also already an artistic construction. The surrounding images make that construction explicit rather than allowing the spectator to forget it.

The painting’s sophistication lies in making a studio arrangement think about representation without turning into a diagram. The objects retain their weight and strangeness, while the spatial instability keeps the viewer aware that seeing and painting are active, selective processes.

Begin with the cast’s apparent solidity and follow the surfaces that seem to support it. At what point does an edge stop resolving the space and start making it uncertain? That question keeps the argument attached to the arrangement. The painting does not require us to abandon the objects in favor of an abstract theory. It makes their weight and their pictorial construction insist on being considered together, leaving the act of seeing unusually exposed.
