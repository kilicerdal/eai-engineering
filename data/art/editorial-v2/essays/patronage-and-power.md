## From an individual patron to an institution | history | 1,2
The Sistine Chapel paintings belong to papal patronage and an architectural program. Rembrandt’s militia and guild portraits represent collective institutions through identifiable people. Neither kind of commission permits a simple opposition between an entirely free artist and an external constraint. Ambition develops through the opportunities and demands of an assignment.

Patrons could supply subject, location and expectations; painters contributed solutions that exceeded a literal list of requirements. The relationship is productive as well as unequal.
## How authority becomes visible | analysis |
Compare Raphael’s School of Athens with Rembrandt’s Sampling Officials. One uses a grand architectural order to organize intellectual authority. The other makes authority appear in an interrupted meeting, through faces that look up from a table. Grandeur and attentiveness offer different means of persuasion.

Then consider Goya’s royal family. Rank appears through costume and grouping, yet individual bodies remain particular. Official representation does not have to remove all human awkwardness to serve its purpose.
## Questions that change the encounter | interpretation |
Ask who was expected to see the painting, from what distance and under what circumstances. Consider whose identity is recorded and whose labor remains unnamed. Notice whether the image presents authority as inherited, learned, sacred or publicly performed.

These questions do not diminish artistic achievement by explaining its conditions. They make achievement more precise. A painter’s invention is often clearest when one understands the problem the commission actually posed.
