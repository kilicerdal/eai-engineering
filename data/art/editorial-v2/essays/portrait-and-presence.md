> Portraits construct encounters. Their capacity to feel intimate does not mean they disclose a sitter’s private character without mediation.

## Identification changes the question | history | 1,2 | section-1

The Louvre identifies the Mona Lisa with Lisa Gherardini. Girl with a Pearl Earring is classified as a tronie, a different kind of figure study. Both can feel like direct personal encounters, but they offer different kinds of historical evidence.

A name supplies context; its absence does not make a painting less human. Conversely, knowing a sitter’s name does not grant access to every thought apparently suggested by a face.

## How a presence is made | analysis |  | section-2

Leonardo uses soft transitions, a turned torso and stable hands to produce a figure who seems responsive without fixing her expression completely. Raphael’s Castiglione uses restricted color and measured posture to make cultivated reserve persuasive. Vermeer withholds environment and lets highlights and a turning head carry the address.

These are distinct constructions of presence. Their effects can be examined through edges, scale, gaze and posture rather than explained by the single word psychological.

## Public image and private inference | interpretation |  | section-3

A portrait can represent rank, friendship, professional identity or an ideal of conduct. The sitter and painter may both participate in that construction. The result can remain moving without becoming a transparent document of personality.

The useful question is not simply whether a face looks sad or confident. It is how the painting encourages that response, what historical circumstances support it, and where the viewer’s own projection begins.
