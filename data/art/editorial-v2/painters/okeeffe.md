# Georgia O’Keeffe: close observation and abstraction can belong to the same act of attention.

> O’Keeffe developed a sustained practice across painting, drawing and pastel. Her repeated subjects do not make the works interchangeable: scale, contour and the particular relation between a form and its surrounding field produce distinct encounters. This opening selection concentrates on the early New York years, not the whole of her later New Mexico practice, and makes room for a work on paper beside the canvases.

## An independent practice within a network | history | 1

O’Keeffe studied and taught art before establishing herself in New York. Her abstract drawings brought her work to Alfred Stieglitz’s attention in 1916, and she moved to New York in 1918. Their professional and personal relationship was important to her visibility, but it should not replace an account of her own decisions. Training, teaching, correspondence and exhibition activity belong to a network through which a sustained practice developed. Independence does not mean that an artist worked without relationships; it means those relationships must not swallow her agency.

## Observation and abstraction | analysis |

A flower can remain recognizable while the enlargement of its folds makes it function as an encompassing space. A storm can be distilled into a sharp event within a broad field. In Blue and Green Music, directional relationships can be followed without identifying one stable depicted object. These approaches do not require a simple progression from realism to abstraction. They show that recognition and formal relationships can be adjusted within a practice, and that the same question about boundaries may lead to quite different results.

## Materials do different work | technical | 1

A Storm is a pastel on paper mounted on board, while the other selected works are oils on canvas. The distinction should remain visible rather than treating all images as equivalent surfaces. Pastel’s soft density and the oil paintings’ transitions suggest different forms of handling, but technical conclusions require more than a screen view. The material record gives a starting point for comparison. It does not authorize a precise reconstruction of working sequences or the assumption that all similar effects were produced by the same means.

## Reception without a single imposed metaphor | interpretation |

O’Keeffe’s flower images have frequently been interpreted through bodily and sexual associations. Such readings belong to their reception; they are not a substitute for the artist’s own statements or for looking at individual paintings. Ask what an interpretation explains about contour, scale and the relation of dark to light. A useful account can acknowledge a history of associations without making one association the essence of an entire career. This is particularly important when a woman artist’s formal decisions have been repeatedly overshadowed by narratives about personality or gender.

## What this selection does and does not represent | history | 1

The three works here were made before the later New Mexico subjects that became central to O’Keeffe’s public identity. The Met’s biography places her first extended visit there in 1929 and permanent move in 1949. This small selection therefore offers an early-career focus rather than a complete retrospective. That boundary is intentional. It enables close comparison among abstraction, flower and atmospheric event, while leaving the later landscapes and bones as a clearly named area for future development rather than pretending that three images cover seven decades.

Compare the pastel surface of A Storm with the modeled contours of Black Iris. A shared interest in natural forms does not imply an identical process or a single intended meaning. Medium and scale should remain part of the account, even when a screen makes the objects appear equally sized.
