# Picasso · who controls the encounter?

> Picasso repeatedly changed the terms on which a painted body could be recognized. Les Demoiselles d’Avignon and Girl before a Mirror offer two consequential arrangements of that problem: confrontation across a compressed space, and identity divided by incompatible appearances.

## A professional practice | history | 1,2

Picasso’s career resists a single signature. A painter trained in academic drawing could make a figure insistently sculptural, dismantle it into facets, or return to an apparently classical contour. These decisions were not a clean march from realism toward abstraction. Different pictorial languages coexisted, and their coexistence asks us to reconsider what stylistic consistency should mean. The two works selected here make that question concrete: the confrontation of a large group in 1907 and the intimate, unstable reflection of a single figure in 1932.

## History and the stories we inherit | history | 1,2

The social setting matters as much as the formal invention. Paris offered exhibitions, dealers, collectors and encounters with objects displaced through colonial networks. Calling African sculpture merely an influence risks treating distinct makers and cultures as anonymous material for European innovation. A responsible account recognizes that asymmetry while examining what Picasso changed on the canvas. Nor should a celebrated male artist’s biography be allowed to erase the agency of women represented in his work. A sitter is a historical person as well as an occasion for pictorial invention.

## A way of looking | analysis | 1,2

Look at edges before trying to decode symbols. A sharp boundary may turn a face into something like a mask; a curved enclosure may make a body appear alternately full and flat. The viewer is asked to assemble a figure without being given a single stable optical position. This is not defective perspective. It is a designed conflict between recognition and the means by which recognition occurs.

## The limits of a signature | analysis | 1,2

These studies deliberately avoid a total biography organized around heroic breakthroughs. They ask who could see the paintings, how museums later established their importance, and what is lost when a complicated career becomes a parade of masterpieces. The works can be admired for their invention while the conditions of their making and reception remain open to scrutiny.

## Two works as a deliberate pairing | analysis | 1,2

A useful comparison begins with the different kinds of doubling in these studies. In Les Demoiselles d’Avignon, the representation of a body can seem to occupy incompatible positions. In Girl before a Mirror, a familiar device for doubling a sitter becomes an independent pictorial field. One problem concerns the consistency of a depicted body; the other concerns the consistency of reflection. They should not be merged into a general claim that Picasso distorted appearances for the same reason throughout his career.

The professional question is therefore not whether a viewer can recognize the artist immediately. It is whether the choices of a particular work can be described closely enough to sustain an argument. Recognition of a style can begin an encounter, but should not end it. Return to a contour, a juxtaposition or a change of orientation that a broad label such as Cubism leaves insufficiently explained.

## A first encounter worth extending | analysis | 1,2

Return to the relation between the standing and seated figures in the 1907 canvas, then to the reach toward the mirror in 1932. In one, a body and its address seem difficult to reconcile; in the other, a hand proposes contact across a difference of depiction. These are useful starting points for a reader who wants more than a chronology of styles. Write an explanation that names a visible boundary and states what it does. Then ask what historical fact would strengthen that explanation. A formal observation can remain persuasive while the larger historical account is still incomplete, provided that the distinction is kept explicit.

## Professional circumstances and public identity | history | 3

MoMA’s history traces Les Demoiselles through Picasso’s studios, Jacques Doucet’s collection and its own acquisition. Dealers and museum directors helped turn a private studio experiment into a public landmark. These institutions did not make the picture, but they changed who could encounter it and the authority attached to it.

## What a movement label leaves out | analysis | 1,2

Cubism helps identify a challenge to consistent representation, but Girl before a Mirror should not be treated as the predictable result of a single Cubist formula.

## Confrontation and reflection | analysis | 1,2

In the 1907 group, disagreement occurs within the bodies and between their different descriptive languages. In the 1932 mirror painting, disagreement is staged between a figure and another image of her. The first compresses an encounter with several figures; the second makes looking at oneself the subject of an unstable exchange. Compare what a contour separates before deciding that both paintings simply distort reality.
