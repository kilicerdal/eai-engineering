> Velázquez’s career joined extraordinary pictorial economy to service within the Spanish royal household. His images create a strong sense of encounter while exposing differences of status, access and visibility. Studying his achievement also requires attention to workshop labor and to Juan de Pareja as a historical person and painter, rather than treating everyone around the master as background to his reputation.

## Training and a change of institution | history | 1 | section-1

Velázquez trained with Francisco Pacheco in Seville and established a practice there before entering Philip IV’s service in Madrid in 1623. The National Gallery’s biography connects his early work with religious subjects and scenes of everyday life. Court employment changed his access to patrons, collections and professional advancement. It should not be treated merely as a reward outside the paintings. The institutional setting helped determine who could be represented, where the resulting objects would go and what kinds of authority images could exercise.

## Seeing through economical marks | technical | 1 | section-2

In the selected works, detail is not evenly distributed. A few accents may carry the appearance of cloth or a face, while nearby passages remain comparatively open. The effect must be considered at the scale of the original object and from a suitable viewing distance. Digital enlargement is useful but can make the marks seem detached from the figure they construct. Technical investigations are needed to establish actual revisions or layer sequences. Visual economy alone does not prove that a picture was produced without planning or difficulty.

## The court and the painter’s position | interpretation |  | section-3

Las Meninas brings the painter into the space of the royal household, making his work and the viewer’s location part of the encounter. The scene raises questions about the standing of painting, but those questions remain embedded in relations of power. Administrative appointments and royal access were elements of a professional life, not evidence that the artist stood outside the household’s hierarchy. His self-representation is particularly compelling because apparent intimacy and institutional distance can coexist within the same convincing room.

## Pareja must not become a footnote | history | 1 | section-4

Juan de Pareja was enslaved in the painter’s household and workshop. The Met’s scholarship identifies the conditions of the 1650 portrait and the papers that provided for Pareja’s freedom by 1654. His subsequent work belongs to an artistic career of his own. A biography of Velázquez should not make the sitter’s visual force into a sentimental story in which a portrait creates someone’s humanity. The inequality is documented; the exact private feelings of the people involved are not available simply by looking at the canvas.

## Making, showing and later reception | analysis |  | section-5

The Spinners places material work beside a distant scene associated with artistic competition. Las Meninas places a hidden working canvas beside a reflected royal presence. Juan de Pareja offers a concentrated encounter whose historical conditions complicate aesthetic praise. Together they show why Velázquez’s sophistication cannot be reduced to lifelike likeness. The pictures make the means, social relations and limits of representation available for thought. Later admiration is one chapter of their reception, but close description remains necessary if that admiration is to become an argument.

The three selected works also expose different settings for looking: a royal room, a portrait made during an Italian journey, and a mythological narrative organized around work. Their common author does not erase those differences. Begin by asking what kind of encounter each painting offers and whose position makes that encounter possible.
