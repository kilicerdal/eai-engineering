# Nobel Stories editorial standard

Use this checklist when researching, revising, or adding a study to the Nobel Stories collection. This is an editorial workflow, not a claim that every existing study has already passed a new review.

## Study structure

1. **Orient the reader.** State the central question, the recognized contribution, and why the story is worth following.
2. **Name the official record.** Record the award year, the formal prize name, recipients, and exact award motivation. Explain date differences when award, announcement, and ceremony years differ.
3. **Follow the work.** Use a dated timeline for the earlier question, major evidence, publication or action, application, and later recognition. Do not make the award year stand in for the whole history.
4. **Describe contributions precisely.** Separate discovery, experimental proof, methods, engineering, organization, clinical translation, funding, and deployment. Do not imply that the laureate list is a complete contributor list.
5. **Separate observation, evidence, and interpretation.** Make clear what was directly recorded, what the record supports, and what remains an argument or open question.
6. **Explain impact with limits.** Distinguish the contribution recognized by the award from later applications and social outcomes. Do not treat an award as proof of every later claim.
7. **End with an optional question.** Use prompts to invite close reading and discussion. Keep them ungraded and do not make them a prerequisite for reading.

## Sources and claims

- Attach claim-specific citations to essays, people, and timeline events. A source's presence in the bibliography does not establish every nearby claim.
- Prefer primary records and strong institutional sources for award motivation, dates, and official process. Pair official summaries with primary research or independent scholarship where the interpretation needs it.
- State what each reference supports and what it does not establish. Mark institutional summaries as institutional accounts; they may compress a wider history.
- Record the date a source was consulted. Run the link audit when network access is available. Treat timeouts, access blocks, and failed automated requests as **unverified**, not as proof that a page is dead.
- Recheck links and attribution when a source is replaced, a date changes, or scholarship changes the account. Keep previous review results when a new audit cannot reach the network.
- Use short quotations only when the exact wording matters; give attribution, date, context, a source link, and a note about the quotation's scope. Prefer paraphrase for extended explanation.

## Language and uncertainty

- Use the formal name **Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel** where legal or historical precision matters. The collection may call it **Economic Sciences** in navigation for brevity.
- Distinguish an award year from a ceremony or announcement date.
- Define specialist terms at first use without replacing accurate vocabulary with a misleading simplification.
- Describe disagreement specifically: identify the disputed point, evidence, positions, and why it matters. Do not suggest that every interpretation is equally supported.
- Use “first,” “discovered,” “invented,” and “transformed” only when the cited record supports that scope. Name prior work and collaborators where material.
- Avoid treating a prize as a ranking of human worth, a complete account of a field, or proof that one individual created a complex result alone.

## Visuals, access, and collection scope

- Prefer original explanatory diagrams or images with verified reuse rights. Record creator, source, license, required credit, and any crop or adaptation. Do not publish an archival image merely because it appears in a search result.
- Give each figure a concise caption and an equivalent text description. State when a schematic is conceptual and not an original document, data plot, photograph, or experimental image.
- Use maps only when place is necessary to understand the story. Cite the geographic source and provide a text account of the relevant locations.
- Keep layouts usable with keyboard navigation, screen readers, enlarged text, reduced motion, print, and narrow mobile screens.
- Describe why a study is in the collection. The collection is a curated selection, not a ranking or representative survey. Review its balance across era, region, discipline, language, institutional access, and kinds of contribution before expanding it.

## Before publishing a revision

- [ ] Award year, formal category, recipients, and citation match the official record.
- [ ] The article distinguishes the named contribution from the wider history.
- [ ] Important factual claims have specific supporting citations.
- [ ] Dates on the timeline distinguish work milestones from prize milestones.
- [ ] People and institutions have accurate, sourced roles; gaps in the record are stated.
- [ ] Uncertainty is concrete and proportionate to the evidence.
- [ ] Source review dates and link status are honest; inaccessible requests are not called broken.
- [ ] Visual rights, credits, captions, and text descriptions are recorded.
- [ ] Field navigation, search, related links, reading time, print, keyboard access, and mobile reflow remain clear.
- [ ] A second editorial review checks attribution, tone, and any high-impact factual claim.
- [ ] The revision date and material changes are recorded in the collection's editorial history.
